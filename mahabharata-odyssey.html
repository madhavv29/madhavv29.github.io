Mahabharata and The Odyssey

October 21, 2026

Introduction and Motivation

With Christopher Nolan's latest and arguably most coveted project, The Odyssey (2026), reaching screens earlier this year, I, along with many others, kept theaters packed for weeks after its release. As an avid Nolan fan (The Prestige (2006) being not only my favorite Nolan film but my favorite film of all time, though that is a topic for later), I personally watched the film three times over the course of the summer, enjoying it more each time. Interestingly, however, while browsing and doomscrolling through reels, I found that a large portion of the internet, including both casual viewers and subject experts like Emily Wilson, had made scathing remarks, particularly concerning the "watering-down" of Odysseus's complex character and the poor scriptwriting, which omitted critical portions of the original epic that, to these critics, constituted its whole point. Embarrassingly, I could not attest to either side of the discourse, having never read a translation of Homer's epic myself.

With my research over the summer wrapping up, I decided to pick up a copy of Fagles's translation to kill time in the couple of weeks before I returned to school. However, in the process of reading The Odyssey, I began to find myself thinking back often to a story I've been told and have read countless times: Vyasa's Mahabharata (for those unaware, the Mahabharata is an ancient Indian epic surrounding a dynastic struggle for a throne, attributed to a legendary, possibly composite author, Vyasa, much like Homer). I couldn't help but notice striking similarities between the two epics, from overarching themes of fate versus free will, duty versus morality, and archaic laws of hospitality to much smaller resemblances involving archery, exile, and visits to the underworld.

My somewhat sparse knowledge of ancient Indian and Greek history led me to instinctively believe that The Odyssey must have had some influence on the composition of the Mahabharata, as Alexander the Great's invasion of India in 326 BCE is hypothesized to have occurred during the period in which the Mahabharata was being composed. However, a few quick searches told me that historians generally view these similarities as the result of common Proto-Indo-European roots and fundamentally human storytelling motifs. Nevertheless, I felt that the commonalities were too striking to be chalked up to shared ancestry and polygenesis alone. Hence, I have chosen to write about the similarities that I noticed.

Note: I will be using Robert Fagles's translation of the Odyssey, and C. Rajagopalchari's (Rajaji's) translation of Mahabharata for references, as they were my original source materials

Exile and Homecoming

Possibly one of the most immediately apparent similarities between the epics is that they both revolve around a common premise: a rightful ruler is unjustly (though not entirely undeservedly) separated from his kingdom and hence must endure a prolonged period rife with suffering and challenges before reclaiming his throne. While this alone is not a strong enough link, it serves as a good jumping-off point when we begin to compare the two works.

In The Odyssey, after the successful invasion and subsequent fall of Troy at the hands of the Greeks, Odysseus, as a result of bad luck and hubris, ends up spending ten years wandering the Mediterranean, facing everything from man-eating giants and enchantresses to the seemingly endless wrath of Poseidon. Quite similarly, in the Mahabharata, the five Pandava brothers are forced into thirteen years of exile after Yudhishthira, the eldest of the five brothers, squanders his kingdom to the Kauravas (their evil counterparts and cousins) in a game of dice manipulated by the cunning Sakuni. Twelve of these years must be spent in the forest, and the final year in disguise, with the condition that discovery would force them to repeat their exile. What stood out to me in particular was the fact that neither exile is entirely undeserved when we take into account the actions of the protagonists themselves.

Odysseus's predicament is often exacerbated throughout his journey by his own arrogance and hubris. Having escaped the Cyclops Polyphemus (in whose cave he had found himself trapped), Odysseus cannot resist revealing his true identity to the blinded monster, despite his companions' pleas to remain silent (one of the “crucial” details that critics of Nolan's film seem to bring up, as it was left out of the film). The Cyclops, as a result of Odysseus's taunts and revealed identity, calls upon his father, Poseidon, to ensure that Odysseus returns home late, alone, and miserable.

On the flip side, Yudhishthira's “flaws” are his weakness for gambling and his overly trusting nature, which cause him to recklessly wager his kingdom and even his wife to the Kauravas during the infamous “wager.” Rajagopalachari describes him as a man whose judgment is at war with his desires. Both Yudhishthira's susceptibility to temptation and Odysseus's hubris serve as blemishes in their otherwise seemingly perfect portrayals, and these character flaws are the root causes of our protagonists' troubles throughout the rest of the stories.

In Book I, Zeus complains, “Ah how shameless—the way these mortals blame the gods,” before arguing that humans frequently compound their suffering through their own recklessness. It is an almost perfect description of both Odysseus and Yudhishthira.

A Bow and a Bride

Of all the parallels I happened to notice, none were quite as visually striking as the archery contests.

In Chapter 17 of Rajaji's Mahabharata, King Drupada, one of the many kings in a then-fragmented India, organizes a swayamvara, a ceremony to find a suitable royal suitor for his daughter, Princess Draupadi, in which her prospective husbands try to demonstrate exceptional martial skill. This particular contest involves a magnificent bow, which a suitor must string before shooting five arrows through the opening of a revolving mechanism to strike a target placed above.

One after another, the prospective suitors (mainly princes and kings) attempt the challenge, only to be met with despair. Among the contestants are some of the most formidable and reputable warriors of the age, including Duryodhana (one of the Kauravas), Jarasandha, and Karna. After murmurs and shouts of discontent from the suitors over a seemingly impossible task, Arjuna (one of the Pandavas), disguised as a brahmana, manages to string the bow effortlessly and strike the target with all five arrows.

Now let’s take a look at Book XXI of The Odyssey.

Penelope (Odysseus's wife, who has faithfully waited for his return), having exhausted her means of perpetually delaying a remarriage actively pushed for by suitors aiming to take Odysseus's vacant throne, announces a contest involving her husband's enormous bow, one famous for being impossible for anyone but Odysseus himself to string. The suitors are asked to string the bow and shoot an arrow through twelve axe heads arranged in a straight line. Predictably, the assembled suitors fail even to string the bow, let alone complete the challenge. In a similar fashion to Arjuna, Odysseus, disguised as an elderly beggar, takes the bow and, stringing it with ease, lets an arrow fly through all twelve axes.

The resemblance is eerily precise. In both cases, an insurmountably difficult archery contest involves a famous bow, with a royal marriage sought after by many at stake; the most accomplished men in the room fail; and the true hero, whose identity is concealed, succeeds with an ease that humiliates the other suitors. The eeriness doesn't end there: most tellingly, neither contest concludes without violence. Arjuna's victory in the swayamvara provokes outrage among the shamed princes, nearly resulting in a battle, while Odysseus's homecoming victory is followed by the slaughter of all the suitors occupying his home.

Becoming a Nobody

It does not end with just the archery contests. As mentioned briefly before, each epic places a decent amount of narrative importance on deception and the necessity for heroes to appear insignificant for the sake of survival.

In the final year of their exile, as part of their agreement with the Kauravas to go incognito for the thirteenth year, the Pandavas seek employment in the court of King Virata. Yudhishthira takes on the guise of a learned courtier skilled in dice (quite ironic, considering it was his lack of skill that led to their predicament), Bhima becomes a cook, and Arjuna disguises himself as a woman, playing the role of a dance and music instructor. The remaining brothers, Nakula and Sahadeva, disguise themselves as a horsekeeper and a cowherd, respectively, while Draupadi disguises herself as the personal maid of Queen Sudeshna, Virata's wife. Odysseus faces a similar situation upon returning to his kingdom, Ithaca. Athena disguises him as an old beggar, allowing him to enter his own household without alerting the suitors while also enabling him to discern those who stayed loyal from those who exploited his absence.

While the Pandavas' disguises were a result of their original wager and predicament, and Odysseus had no such constraint, he was also, in some sense, “forced” to adopt a disguise. Without it, he might have been slain before he set foot in his castle by eager suitors who learned of his arrival (this is not a totally unjustified assumption, as they also plotted to kill Telemachus, Odysseus's son, while he was on a voyage). His disguise also allowed the suitors to become “fair game” to kill, as they openly disrespected him without knowing he was disguised as a common person (though the morality of this is questionable; going by the archaic standards, we can presume that he would not have had grounds to kill the suitors without witnessing their abuse of his home and wife). Nevertheless, both epics use the importance of disguise as a device to further the plot, particularly in the twilight stages of each story.

Gods and the Questionable Rules of Fair Play

If the bows and archery contests represent the most visually striking similarity between the two works, the relationship between their protagonists and the divine represents the most philosophically interesting one, in my opinion.

Athena maintains an unusually intimate relationship with Odysseus. At times, she is shown to admire him (particularly his intelligence), repeatedly protecting him through divine intervention, manipulating his physical appearance, and even helping to plan the destruction of the suitors (a morally very questionable thing for a god to partake in). Their reunion in Book XIII reveals a playful side to their relationship, with Athena recognizing in Odysseus the same fondness for cunning wit that characterizes her own actions.

Similarly, Krishna (one of the many incarnations of the Hindu god Vishnu) plays an indispensable role in the Pandavas' successes throughout the events of the Mahabharata, though he has greater theological significance. Toward the end of the Mahabharata, war breaks out between the Pandavas and the Kauravas, with neighboring kings aligning themselves with one side or the other. For the duration of the war, Krishna agrees to be Arjuna's charioteer and divine guide, advising the Pandavas on various matters, from political negotiation to the conduct of battle. His greatest impact, however, comes when Arjuna begins to falter and hesitate at the prospect of fighting his own kin, teachers, and friends (who, by blood or by duty, end up fighting for the Kauravas). Krishna then intervenes and provides the philosophical instruction of the Bhagavad Gita. The Gita is a Hindu religious text in the form of a conversation between Krishna and Arjuna, which expands into a deep philosophical discourse on duty, the nature of existence, the soul, and the relationship between humanity and the divine. The parallel between the epics seems straightforward at first: divine patrons who ensure that their favored heroes ultimately prevail.

I, however, found the more interesting thematic commonality to be that both Athena and Krishna permit, and sometimes actively encourage, actions that complicate our understanding of morality.

Let us take a look at the death of Drona (a teacher to both the Kauravas and Pandavas during their childhood, bound by duty to fight for the Kauravas) in the Mahabharata. Unable to defeat their beloved former teacher through conventional warfare (Drona was touted as one of the best warriors of the time), the Pandavas exploit his love for his son, Aswatthama. After a battle elephant bearing the same name is killed, Yudhishthira is persuaded by Krishna to announce that Aswatthama has died, adding that it was the elephant in much softer words, drowned out by the surrounding noise. Drona, hearing only the first part, believes his son to be dead, loses his will to fight, and is subsequently killed. For Yudhishthira, who was reputed to be solely committed to truth throughout the epic, the deception carries a severe moral consequence. Rajaji describes how his chariot, which had previously levitated slightly above the ground as a result of his righteousness, descends to the earth. “He too desired victory and slipped into the way of untruth,” Rajaji observes in Chapter 90. Similarly, during a confrontation with Arjuna, Karna's chariot wheel gets lodged in the mud, and Krishna urges Arjuna to kill Karna despite the conventions of honorable warfare at the time. Krishna justifies his advice by reminding Karna of his own participation in the numerous dishonorable injustices committed against the Pandavas by him and those he supports.

In both episodes, righteousness and strategic necessity directly conflict with each other.

Athena's interventions raise similar questions. Odysseus's bloody revenge is (somewhat) justified by the suitors' exploitation of his household and mistreatment of his family, yet the goddess does not simply help him restore order through negotiation or a peaceful demonstration of legitimate authority. She actively facilitates the suitors' destruction. In Book XVIII, Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, gets involved in a fight with another beggar named Irus. For the sake of her champion, Athena steps in to artificially augment Odysseus's muscles and stature, actively pushing him to engage a desperate, impoverished man in a fistfight that leads to Irus's broken jaw. However, even this is done with the overarching goal of protecting Odysseus's cover and maintaining his standing in the palace under his disguise as a beggar. Even the act of supporting Odysseus throughout his journey, while others admonish and punish his slyness, shows us Athena prioritizing the values she sees reflected in Odysseus over objective morality.

If a god sanctions an action, does that make it morally righteous?

Neither Homer nor Vyasa offers us an entirely uncomplicated answer. In the Mahabharata, the moral costs of strategic deception are explicitly acknowledged, with a larger, seemingly more “moral” goal in mind. In The Odyssey, the restoration of the household receives stronger divine approval, but the possibility of retaliation reveals that even justified revenge can generate further violence.

Hospitality and the Fragility of Civilization

During the initial conception of my writeup, I underestimated the impact of norms and duty on the social fabric, having seen them primarily as tools in The Odyssey. However, with further rereading, I've deemed it necessary to dedicate a section to comparing the two epics on this topic.

In The Odyssey, hospitality is a moral yardstick.

The distinction between societies considered civilized and uncivilized is repeatedly illustrated through how strangers are received in one's home. The Phaeacians provide Odysseus with shelter and passage home, while the Cyclops Polyphemus offers a rather memorable demonstration of what happens when the obligation to protect guests is disregarded. This core principle is referred to as xenia (guest-friendship).

The suitors represent an inversion of this fundamental norm upon which a large part of ancient Greek society seems to have been predicated. Having entered Odysseus's household as prospective guests and husbands, they abuse xenia and proceed to steal, mistreat his servants and family, and generally abuse the generosity upon which their presence depends. Their violation of Penelope's hospitality is a sign of the collapse of the social order that allows households and communities to function.

Hospitality occupies a similarly important, although differently expressed, position in the Mahabharata.

One demonstration of this is visible in Chapter 41, when the famously irascible sage Durvasa arrives with his disciples at the Pandavas' dwelling during their exile. Through Duryodhana's scheming, they find themselves in a position where they must feed Durvasa despite having exhausted their food supply. Fearful of slighting Durvasa and risking his anger, Draupadi appeals to Krishna for help, and he miraculously satisfies Durvasa's hunger by consuming a tiny remnant of food left in the distraught wife's vessel.

While the demonstrations of this principle differ across the stories, the principle at heart is quite visible: the obligation to care for a guest is treated as a sacred duty, the failure of which can carry consequences far beyond ordinary social embarrassment.

In The Odyssey, the abuse of xenia is itself a justification for Odysseus's revenge. In the Mahabharata, hospitality forms part of a much broader structure of dharma governing the responsibilities of hosts, rulers, relatives, teachers, and warriors. The violations of this larger moral order are what ultimately lead to the Kurukshetra War. What both epics emphasize is that civilization depends upon rules that powerful individuals must agree to respect even when violating them would be advantageous. And once those rules cease to bind the powerful, the consequences begin to spiral catastrophically.

Fate, Free Will, and the Illusion of Choice

The final set of similarities I want to discuss concerns the way both epics explore the concepts of fate and free will, particularly how characters, through their actions, bring about a preordained fate and then spend the rest of the story trying to avoid it.

In Book IX of The Odyssey, Polyphemus the Cyclops reveals that a prophet had already foretold that he would be blinded by Odysseus. Ironically, Odysseus, despite his companions' warnings after their escape, reveals his identity out of hubris, prompting Polyphemus to invoke Poseidon's wrath and curse him to the very suffering that plagues the rest of his journey home. His seemingly freely chosen actions become the means through which his fate is ordained.

The Mahabharata presents an even more explicit example of this. In Chapter 105, after being cursed by a group of sages who foretell the demise of their race through an iron mace, the Yadavas grind the mace into powder and scatter it into the sea, trying to escape their fate. As foretold, reeds grow where the powder settles, which later become the very weapons the Yadavas use to slaughter one another. Similarly, Karna, despite warnings from the Sun god (his father) of Indra's intention to acquire his divine armor, willingly surrenders it to uphold his reputation for generosity, further enabling the death toward which his accumulated curses lead him.

Interestingly enough, neither story entirely dismisses the possibility of choosing otherwise or having an element of free will. Tiresias explicitly warns Odysseus that his companions may survive if they spare the cattle of Helios, while Duryodhana repeatedly rejects opportunities to avert the Kurukshetra War, refusing to surrender even five villages to the Pandavas. In both cases, the catastrophes are consequences of decisions the characters could seemingly have avoided.

Conclusion

That concludes my analysis of the two works (for now). I could probably go into further detail about similar character archetypes, like Penelope and Draupadi, who play the roles of enduring, besieged wives: royal women whose husbands' losses of political power have caused them great humiliation and left them vulnerable to exploitation by other men. Both have endured prolonged suffering and remained fiercely committed to their families despite circumstances that would've made surrender considerably more attractive.

I could talk about our protagonists' respective journeys to the underworld/hell (though at different points in their stories). I could talk about how both epics deeply highlight the cost of the ultimate victory achieved. I could even talk about the symbolism of the fact that each had a loyal dog that followed him until the penultimate stage of his story, and how it represents duty and loyalty. Yet I choose not to, partly because the main points I've already made were the more compelling ones, and partly because it is 5:54 AM and I will not be able to sleep until I finish writing this.

Certainly, the similarities remain striking. This is, of course, an interpretive comparison rather than a direct equivalence, but it illustrates how similar narrative problems can be explored through profoundly different characters. There is also an important limitation to my comparison. Fagles's Odyssey is a translation of Homer's poem, whereas Rajagopalachari's Mahabharata is a condensed retelling of a considerably larger epic. Consequently, I am comparing two particular presentations of these stories, rather than attempting an exhaustive comparison of their original literary traditions.

And while the textual similarities are certainly worth exploring, they cannot, by themselves, establish a historical relationship between the works. Perhaps these commonalities do reflect shared cultural ancestry. Perhaps they represent narrative structures that emerged independently in response to recurring human experiences. Or perhaps some combination of these explanations is responsible. I am not sufficiently qualified to settle that question, and I suspect that doing so would require considerably more than just these literary observations made during my free time over the summer.

For now, I think there is something remarkable about two works emerging from civilizations separated by enormous distances and arriving at such similar questions about power, morality, and what it means to be human. And perhaps, regardless of how their similarities came to be, that is reason enough to read them together.
